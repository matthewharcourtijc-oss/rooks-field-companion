FIELD SYSTEMS / UK EDITION

Stay capable.
Think clearly. Adapt.

A practical UK field manual for preventing trouble, managing the essentials and caring for yourself or a companion when help is delayed or unavailable.

Offline reference • Practise skills before relying on them.
Operating principle: prevent, protect, stabilise, reassess. Use the skills and equipment you have; conserve energy, warmth, water and battery. Do not create a second casualty trying to be a hero.
Know the red lines. Severe bleeding, penetrating wounds, chest pain, stroke signs, major breathing difficulty, unconsciousness, anaphylaxis, serious burns, suspected hypothermia or major trauma need urgent professional care. In the UK call 999/112 when life is at risk. Self-reliance means doing the right things immediately—not delaying care that may be lifesaving.

01 / First priorities

Protect yourselfDo not become the next casualty. Check traffic, weapons, fire, electricity, unstable ground, water, animals and ongoing violence. Wait for police or ambulance if the scene is unsafe.
Get help earlyCall 999/112 for life-threatening illness or injury. Use speakerphone. Send someone to guide responders in.
Stop major bleedingSevere external bleeding can kill within minutes. Direct pressure is the first action for most wounds.
Keep them warmInsulate from cold ground, protect from wind and rain, reassure and monitor. Do not give food or drink to someone seriously injured or drowsy.

Simple casualty check

  1. Danger: Is it safe to approach?
  2. Response: Speak loudly, ask their name and gently tap their shoulders.
  3. Airway and breathing: Open the airway if trained; look for normal breathing. Gasping is not normal breathing.
  4. Catastrophic bleeding: Look for major blood loss and control it immediately.
  5. Call and continue: 999/112, follow instructions, keep reassessing.
Do not move a seriously injured person unless they are in immediate danger, need CPR/airway care, or the emergency call handler directs you.

02 / Remote fieldcraft — when help is delayed

Use a simple cycle: STOP → assess → plan → act → reassess. Do not burn energy on panic, wandering or unnecessary jobs.

Stabilise the situationStop movement if lost or uncertain. Check people, injuries, weather, daylight, terrain, water, shelter and communications. Deal with immediate threats before comfort tasks.
Protect the groupKeep everyone together unless a specific task is safer with a buddy. Set a meeting point and return time. Children and injured people stay supervised.
Use resources in orderDry shelter and insulation, safe water, essential medication, food, navigation and signalling. Food is usually less urgent than warmth, water and a safe location.
Make a written planRecord time, location, weather, supplies, injuries, actions taken and next decision point. Reassess whenever conditions change.

If you are lost

  1. Stop. Do not keep walking in the hope that the route will appear. Sit somewhere safe and get out of wind/rain.
  2. Think. Recall the last certain location, direction of travel, time and identifiable landmarks.
  3. Observe. Check map, compass, terrain, daylight, weather and battery. Do not rely on a phone map as your only navigation tool.
  4. Plan. Return only if the route is known and safe. Otherwise shelter in a visible safe place and avoid hazardous shortcuts.
  5. Signal. Use phone, whistle, torch or high-visibility clothing. If there is no signal, conserve battery and try again from a safe open location; do not climb exposed terrain or wander into danger chasing reception.

When there is no mobile signal

  • Move only if the current spot is unsafe or a nearby safer position is obvious. Keep the group together and leave a clear note of direction and time if a split is unavoidable.
  • Try an emergency call from a safe open position; a phone may connect through another available network, but this is not guaranteed.
  • Use any available satellite SOS device according to its instructions. Tell your trusted contact your plan when you still have coverage.
  • Make yourself visible from safe ground. Use repeated whistle blasts or torch flashes with pauses; conserve power and avoid continuous signalling.
  • Do not assume a text, app message or location share has been delivered until confirmed.

Casualty care while evacuation is delayed

  • Deal with immediate threats: catastrophic bleeding, airway obstruction, abnormal or absent breathing, severe allergic reaction and environmental danger.
  • Keep the casualty still, dry, insulated from the ground and protected from wind. Handle gently, especially after a fall or suspected spinal injury.
  • Recheck responsiveness, breathing, bleeding, skin colour/temperature and pain at regular intervals. Record changes and times.
  • Give oral fluids only to someone fully awake, able to swallow normally and not suspected of needing urgent surgery; never give food or drink to someone drowsy, confused, vomiting or seriously injured.
  • Do not attempt invasive procedures, remove embedded objects, explore wounds, reset fractures or give prescription medicines that are not theirs.
Evacuation decision: worsening breathing, altered consciousness, uncontrolled bleeding, penetrating injury, severe pain after major trauma, suspected spinal injury, heatstroke, hypothermia, stroke, heart attack or anaphylaxis are not “walk it off” problems. Arrange urgent evacuation and emergency care. Do not make an injured person walk simply to save effort.

Minor problems you can often manage in the field

Small cuts and grazesWash hands, rinse dirt out with clean potable water, control minor bleeding with pressure, dry surrounding skin and cover with a clean dressing. Change it if wet or dirty. Seek care if redness spreads, pain worsens, pus or fever develops.
BlistersStop the rubbing early. Clean and dry the area, cover with a blister dressing or padding, and adjust footwear/socks. Do not deliberately burst a small intact blister. A hot, red, swollen or draining blister needs assessment.
Minor sprainsStop the activity, protect and support the joint, use a wrapped cool pack for short periods if available, and elevate if comfortable. Do not force movement. Seek assessment for severe pain, deformity, inability to bear weight or numb/cold/blue fingers or toes.
Chafing and foot careClean and dry skin, change damp socks, remove grit, protect hotspots with tape or dressings and check feet daily. Do not keep marching on a worsening wound.

03 / Trauma — gunshot, stab and major wounds

Gunshot or penetrating wound = emergency. Call 999 immediately, even if the wound looks small. Internal damage and bleeding may be much worse than they appear. Ensure the scene is safe; an active threat means withdraw and wait for police.

Major external bleeding

  1. Wear gloves if immediately available; do not delay life-saving pressure to find them.
  2. Expose the wound enough to locate the bleeding. Apply hard, continuous direct pressure with a dressing or clean cloth.
  3. Call 999/112. Use speakerphone and state that it is a penetrating injury with severe bleeding.
  4. If blood soaks through, keep pressure on; add material as directed by the call handler rather than repeatedly lifting the dressing to look.
  5. Once controlled, secure the dressing. Keep the casualty still, warm and reassured; monitor breathing and response.

Tourniquets — life-threatening limb bleeding

  • Use a purpose-made commercial tourniquet for catastrophic arm or leg bleeding when direct pressure is ineffective or impractical, ideally if trained.
  • Place it above the wound, not over a joint; tighten until bleeding stops. A second device may be needed if bleeding continues.
  • Note the application time. Do not loosen or remove it. Only medical professionals should do that.
  • If no tourniquet is available, maintain firm direct pressure and follow ambulance-control instructions. Do not improvise one unless directed by the emergency call handler or appropriately trained.

Gunshot wounds by location

Arm or legControl major bleeding. A commercial tourniquet may be needed for catastrophic limb bleeding. Do not probe for a bullet or fragments. Treat as possible fracture and keep still.
ChestCall 999. Do not probe, pack or plug the hole. Current UK resuscitation guidance says an open chest wound should be left open to communicate with the air; do not seal it with an airtight dressing. Monitor breathing closely.
AbdomenCall 999. Do not press deeply, probe, or push protruding organs back inside. Do not give food or drink. Cover exposed tissue loosely with a sterile dressing if available and follow the call handler.
Head, neck or groinCall 999. Apply careful pressure around an external bleeding point where safe, but never compress the airway or press on an embedded object. Do not pack these areas unless trained and specifically directed.

Stab wounds and embedded objects

  • Do not remove the knife, shard or other embedded object — it may be limiting blood loss.
  • Apply pressure around the object, not on top of it. Stabilise it with bulky dressings if possible.
  • Penetrating injuries to the chest, neck, abdomen or groin are emergencies even when external bleeding is limited.

Shock

Warning signs include pale, cold or clammy skin, rapid breathing, weakness, dizziness, confusion, thirst and collapse. Call 999, treat the cause (especially bleeding), lay them down if tolerated, keep warm, reassure and monitor. Do not give food or drink. Do not raise legs if this worsens pain, breathing or injury.

COMMERCIAL TOURNIQUET — LIMB PLACEMENT

USECatastrophic limb bleeding; direct pressure is ineffective or impractical.
CHECKBleeding has stopped. If it has not, follow device training and emergency call-handler guidance; a second commercial device may be needed.
RECORDWrite down application time and tell ambulance crews.
NEVERLoosen, remove or cover it so it cannot be seen. Do not use a belt, cord or improvised windlass.
Call 999/112 immediately. Do not delay pressure or emergency help to find a kit. A tourniquet is for life-threatening limb bleeding, not minor wounds. Training matters.

04 / Major trauma — field stabilisation and evacuation

In remote terrain, the first-aid job is to prevent deterioration while arranging the safest realistic route to definitive care. You are not trying to perform hospital treatment in the woods.

Control what you canMaintain direct pressure on serious external bleeding. Use a commercial tourniquet or haemostatic dressing only within your training and device instructions. Record the time and never loosen an applied tourniquet.
Protect breathingKeep the airway clear and watch breathing continuously. An open chest wound must not be sealed airtight. Do not probe or pack a chest wound.
Prevent heat lossInsulate underneath and around the casualty, shelter from wind/rain and replace wet layers when practical. Keep them still and handle gently.
Prepare evacuationIdentify the safest access point, hazards, route, helpers and equipment. Share location and casualty status whenever communications allow.

Gunshot and penetrating injuries — hard limits

  • Assume internal injury even if the entry wound is small or bleeding appears limited. A bullet may damage organs, vessels and bone.
  • Do not search for, probe for or remove a projectile or fragments. Do not close a penetrating wound with glue, tape or improvised stitches.
  • For catastrophic limb bleeding, use a purpose-made tourniquet if trained and available; tighten until bleeding stops, secure it and note the time. Do not loosen or remove it.
  • If no tourniquet is available, use firm direct pressure and a suitable haemostatic dressing if trained. Follow current training and emergency-control instructions for any improvised device.
  • For chest wounds, do not plug or seal the opening airtight. Keep the person in the position that makes breathing easiest and monitor closely.
  • For abdominal wounds, do not push exposed tissue back inside or apply deep pressure. Cover loosely with a clean sterile dressing if available.
  • Keep the casualty warm, still and reassured. Do not give food, drink or alcohol. Record wound locations, bleeding control, breathing changes and times.
These injuries require urgent emergency care even if the casualty seems stable. Do not use apparent improvement as a reason to cancel evacuation. A gunshot or stab wound can deteriorate suddenly.

05 / First aid essentials

Unresponsive and not breathing normally — CPR
  1. Call 999/112, speakerphone, send for an AED.
  2. Start chest compressions in the centre of the chest: hard and fast, 100–120 per minute, about 5–6 cm deep in an adult.
  3. If trained and willing, give 30 compressions then 2 rescue breaths. If not, give continuous chest compressions and follow the call handler.
  4. Use an AED as soon as it arrives. Switch it on and follow its spoken prompts. Do not touch the casualty during analysis or shock.
Choking — adult
  • If they can cough, encourage coughing.
  • If they cannot speak, breathe or cough effectively, call 999. Give up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades.
  • If ineffective, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until cleared or help arrives.
  • If they become unresponsive, lower them safely and start CPR. Do not perform blind finger sweeps.
  • Anyone who has needed abdominal thrusts should receive medical assessment.
Burns and scalds
  • Cool under cool running water for 20 minutes as soon as possible.
  • Remove jewellery and loose clothing near the burn; do not pull away anything stuck to skin.
  • After cooling, loosely cover with cling film laid lengthways or a sterile non-fluffy dressing. Do not wrap tightly.
  • Do not use ice, butter, creams or adhesive dressings. Call 999 for large/deep burns, face, hands, genitals, major joints, chemical or electrical burns.
Broken bones and sprains
  • Support the injury in the position found; keep it still. Do not try to straighten a deformity or push bone back in.
  • For a closed injury, use a wrapped cold pack for short periods if tolerated.
  • For open fractures, cover loosely with a sterile dressing; control bleeding around the wound.
  • Call 999 for major deformity, severe pain, loss of sensation/circulation, suspected spinal injury or inability to move safely.
Hypothermia — dangerous cooling
  • Signs: shivering, cold/pale skin, slurred speech, clumsiness, unusual tiredness, confusion or slow breathing.
  • Call 999 if hypothermia is suspected. Move to shelter, replace wet clothing if possible, insulate and cover the head.
  • Handle gently. Give a warm sweet non-alcoholic drink only if fully awake and able to swallow.
  • No hot bath, direct intense heat, rubbing limbs or alcohol. Keep monitoring.
Heat exhaustion and heatstroke
  • Move to shade/cool place, remove excess clothing, cool skin with water and fan. Give small sips of cool water if fully alert.
  • Heatstroke is an emergency: confusion, seizure, collapse or very high temperature. Call 999 and cool rapidly while waiting. Do not give drinks to someone confused or unconscious.
Seizure
  • Protect from nearby hazards, cushion the head and note the time. Do not restrain them or put anything in their mouth.
  • When convulsions stop, check breathing and place in recovery position if breathing normally.
  • Call 999 for a first seizure, one lasting 5 minutes or more, repeated seizures without recovery, injury, breathing difficulty or seizure in water.
Recovery position — unconscious but breathing normally
  • Call 999 and monitor breathing continuously.
  • If there is no reason to suspect spinal injury, place them on their side with the airway open and mouth angled down so fluid can drain.
  • If major trauma or spinal injury is suspected, avoid unnecessary movement, but airway and breathing take priority. Follow the call handler.
  • If breathing becomes abnormal or stops, roll them onto their back and start CPR.
Anaphylaxis — severe allergic reaction
  • Warning signs include throat/tongue swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, wheeze, collapse, faintness or sudden confusion.
  • Use the person's prescribed adrenaline auto-injector immediately, following the device instructions. Call 999 and say “anaphylaxis”.
  • Help them lie flat with legs raised if possible. If breathing is difficult, they may need to sit supported; do not let them stand or walk.
  • If symptoms have not improved after 5 minutes and a second prescribed auto-injector is available, use it according to its instructions. They need hospital assessment even if they improve.
Stroke — FAST
  • Face: one side droops when smiling?
  • Arms: can they raise both arms and keep them up?
  • Speech: slurred, confused or unable to speak normally?
  • Time: call 999 immediately. Note when they were last known well. Do not give food, drink or medication. Symptoms that improve still need emergency assessment.
Heart attack
  • Possible signs: chest pressure/pain, pain spreading to arms, jaw, neck or back, breathlessness, sweating, nausea or collapse.
  • Call 999. Sit and rest in a comfortable supported position; keep calm and monitor breathing.
  • Follow the call handler's advice about aspirin or prescribed angina medication. Do not drive them yourself.
  • If they become unresponsive and are not breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED.
Electric shock
  • Do not touch the casualty while they may still be in contact with electricity.
  • Switch off the power at the mains if safe. For high-voltage lines, stay well clear and call 999; do not approach until the electricity authority confirms it is safe.
  • Once safe, call 999 for significant shock, burns, collapse, chest pain or abnormal breathing. Start CPR/AED if needed and safe.
  • Electrical burns may be small on the surface but hide deeper injury. Medical assessment is important.
Animal bites and stings
  • Move away from the animal and make the scene safe. For bites, wash thoroughly with soap and running water and cover with a clean dressing.
  • Seek prompt medical advice for any bite that breaks the skin, especially on the hand, face, near a joint, or if infection risk is high. Check tetanus advice.
  • Call 999 for anaphylaxis, severe bleeding, collapse or breathing difficulty. Use a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector if indicated.
Poisoning and suspected toxic fungi
  • Call 999 for collapse, seizure, breathing difficulty or severe symptoms. Otherwise seek urgent NHS 111 advice.
  • Do not induce vomiting. Do not give food, drink, milk or charcoal unless a clinician tells you to.
  • Keep packaging or a sample/photo of the substance or fungus for clinicians, without handling it unnecessarily.
  • Some mushroom toxins cause delayed symptoms. Do not wait for symptoms after a suspected ingestion.
Wounds, blisters and infection
  • Small cuts: rinse with clean running water, dry surrounding skin and cover with a sterile dressing.
  • Do not remove deeply embedded material. Seek medical help for deep, dirty, bite or puncture wounds, uncontrolled bleeding, or signs of infection.
  • Check tetanus status after dirty/deep wounds; ask NHS 111 or a clinician.

06 / Shelter and insulation

Priority is protection from wind, rain and ground heat loss. A small, dry, well-insulated shelter is often better than a large elaborate one.

A-frame tarpGood general rain and wind cover. Tension the ridge, peg corners and angle the low end into the wind.
Lean-to tarpUseful when conditions allow a windbreak. Face the open side away from prevailing wind.

Site selection

  • Avoid river banks, dry stream beds, gullies, flood plains and low hollows.
  • Avoid lone trees in storms, dead branches overhead, unstable slopes and rockfall zones.
  • Stay clear of roads, railways, power lines, livestock and private property.
  • Insulate yourself from the ground with a mat, dry vegetation or spare clothing. Keep ventilation and an exit.

07 / Fire and heat

Fire is optional; staying warm and dry without one is often safer. Never light fires where prohibited, during high fire risk, near dry vegetation, peat, buildings or livestock.
  1. Choose a clear, legal site away from overhanging branches, roots, tents and fuel stores.
  2. Prepare tinder, pencil-thin kindling and progressively larger dry fuel before lighting.
  3. Keep the fire small and attended. Have water or soil ready to extinguish it.
  4. Never use petrol, spirits or other accelerants. Do not burn treated wood, plastics or rubbish.
  5. Extinguish completely: drown, stir, feel for residual heat only when safe, and repeat until cold. Leave no trace.

08 / Water

Untreated surface water can carry pathogens and chemical pollution. Clear, fast-flowing or mountain water is not automatically safe.

Safer approach

  • Prefer sealed bottled water or a known safe supply.
  • Collect from the cleanest available source, upstream of camps, animals, drains and human activity. Avoid algal blooms, stagnant water, industrial runoff, fuel sheen or chemical smells.
  • Let sediment settle and pre-filter through clean cloth if needed. This does not disinfect.
  • Boil at a rolling boil for 1 minute; at elevations above 2,000 m, boil for 3 minutes. Cool and store covered in a clean container.
  • A suitable certified filter can reduce many organisms, but filters do not reliably remove viruses, salts, fuel or dissolved chemicals. Follow its specification; combine methods where appropriate.
Cloth pre-filterNot a treatment step.
BoilingRolling boil; keep covered while cooling.

BUILD A SIMPLE GRAVITY PRE-FILTER

A field-made sand-and-gravel filter can reduce visible sediment and improve clarity. It does not make water microbiologically safe and cannot remove dissolved chemicals, fuel, salts or reliably remove viruses.

1 — SettleLet muddy water stand; pour off the clearer upper layer without disturbing sludge.
2 — Pre-filterPass through clean cloth, then the gravel/sand layers if available.
3 — DisinfectBoil at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m), or use an approved treatment exactly as labelled.
4 — Store cleanCool covered; pour into a clean, closed container. Keep untreated water and dirty hands away.
Critical: If the source may contain fuel, pesticides, industrial chemicals, saltwater or blue-green algae toxins, do not drink it. A homemade filter and boiling will not make it safe. Find another source.

09 / Hygiene, sanitation and illness prevention

Preventing diarrhoea, infected wounds and contaminated water is easier than treating them without supplies.

Hand hygieneWash with soap and safe water after toilet use, before food preparation/eating and after handling waste, animals or wounds. If water is scarce, use hand sanitiser on visibly clean hands; it does not replace washing when hands are dirty.
Toilet wasteUse established toilets where available. Otherwise follow local land rules and site guidance; keep human waste well away from watercourses, lakes, paths and camps, and pack out waste where required. Never contaminate a water source.
Food safetyKeep raw and cooked food separate, wash hands and utensils, cook food thoroughly and discard food that has been warm for too long or smells/looks spoiled. Do not eat unknown wild foods.
Wound cleanlinessUse clean water and clean dressings. Keep dressings dry and replace dirty ones. Do not pour alcohol, fuel, bleach or household disinfectant into wounds.

Reduce illness risk

  • Keep drinking vessels separate from containers used for untreated water.
  • Store rubbish and food waste sealed and away from sleeping areas; do not attract rodents or wildlife.
  • Protect cuts, wash bites promptly and check for ticks after walking through vegetation. Remove ticks with a proper tick remover or fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin; do not burn or smother them.
  • Keep wet clothing, socks and footwear aired when possible. Dry feet and check for hotspots before they become injuries.
  • Use a clean barrier or gloves when dealing with blood, vomit or faeces; wash thoroughly afterwards.
Persistent vomiting/diarrhoea, blood in stool, severe dehydration, fever with confusion, worsening wound infection or inability to keep fluids down needs medical advice. Do not rely on improvised antibiotics or unverified remedies.

10 / Food, plants and foraging

Never eat a wild plant, berry or fungus unless its identity is certain and confirmed by a competent expert. This manual is not an edible-species key. One wrong identification can cause organ failure or death.
  • Do not taste-test, nibble, or rely on smell, colour, animal feeding, silver spoons or folk rules.
  • Do not identify from one photo or app. Plants vary with age, season and growing conditions.
  • Avoid collecting beside roads, industrial sites, sprayed fields, dog-walking areas or polluted water.
  • Respect land access rules, protected species and conservation sites. Take only what is legal and sustainable.
  • In a survival emergency, prioritise rescue, shelter, water and signalling over risky foraging.

Plant identification checklist — observe, do not consume

  • Whole plant and growth habit; leaf arrangement and shape; leaf edges and veins.
  • Stem, sap, hairs, thorns and smell (do not deliberately inhale unknown plants).
  • Flowers, fruit, seeds and roots only where lawful and without destructive digging.
  • Habitat, soil, nearby trees, season and location.
  • Compare multiple independent features with a trusted regional field guide; if anything does not match, leave it.

11 / UK mushroom and fungi awareness

Look, photograph and leave. Do not eat fungi based on this guide or any online image. Fungi identification often requires multiple features, spore print, microscopy or expert examination. Some deadly species resemble edible ones.

Stem and baseRing, volva, bulb, fibres, colour and full base (do not destroy protected habitat).

If someone may have eaten a wild fungus

  1. Call 999 for collapse, seizure, breathing problems or severe symptoms; otherwise seek urgent NHS 111 advice immediately.
  2. Do not wait for symptoms — some serious toxins have delayed effects.
  3. Do not induce vomiting or give food, drink, milk or remedies unless instructed by a clinician.
  4. Keep a photo and any remaining sample in a separate container for clinicians; wash hands after handling.
  5. Record when and how much may have been eaten, and whether others ate it too.

It cannot confirm a species, prove a plant is safe, or replace expert examination. Species vary with age, season, weather and location. Similar-looking harmless and poisonous species can grow side by side. Never taste-test, nibble, cook or use an app/photo match to decide something is edible. When uncertain: leave it untouched.

Dangerous mushrooms and toadstools

Death cap

Amanita phalloides · potentially fatal

Appearance to be aware of: cap can be olive, yellow-green, bronze, brown or pale; white gills; pale stem, often with a ring and a sac-like cup at the swollen base.

Where: often beneath broadleaf trees, especially oak and beech; summer to autumn.

Hazard: amatoxins can cause severe liver and kidney injury and death. Early stomach symptoms may ease before serious organ damage becomes apparent.

Destroying angel

Amanita virosa · potentially fatal

Appearance to be aware of: entirely white cap, gills and stem; may have a ring and a bag-like volva at the base. Young specimens can be egg-shaped.

Where: woodland, including birch and mixed broadleaf sites; late summer to autumn.

Hazard: amatoxins cause potentially fatal liver injury. Its pale appearance can be mistaken for edible mushrooms.

Open expert photo reference ↗

Deadly webcap

Cortinarius rubellus · potentially fatal

Appearance to be aware of: dry rusty-orange to reddish-brown cap, often with a central point; similarly coloured stem and rusty gills.

Where: conifer woods, often among moss or heather; autumn.

Hazard: orellanine can cause severe kidney damage. Symptoms may be delayed for days or longer. Do not assume feeling well means no danger.

Open expert photo reference ↗

Funeral bell

Galerina marginata · potentially fatal

Appearance to be aware of: small honey-brown to orange-brown caps, often in clusters on dead wood; pale to brown stems with a ring zone that may fade.

Where: dead stumps, logs and decaying wood in woodland; summer and autumn.

Hazard: contains amatoxins, the same major toxin group found in death caps. It can be confused with other small brown wood-growing fungi.

Open expert photo reference ↗

Fool's funnel

Clitocybe rivulosa / dealbata · toxic

Appearance to be aware of: small pale cream or whitish cap, often shallowly funnel-shaped; crowded gills may run down the stem.

Where: lawns, meadows and grassy areas; can appear in rings during autumn.

Hazard: muscarine poisoning can cause heavy sweating, salivation, tears, vomiting, diarrhoea, slow heart rate and breathing difficulty. It may grow near edible-looking grassland species.

Open expert photo reference ↗

Panther cap

Amanita pantherina · toxic

Appearance to be aware of: brown cap often carrying pale veil patches, white gills and stem, with a ring and swollen base. Patches can wash off.

Where: broadleaf woodland, especially with beech or oak; summer to autumn.

Hazard: ibotenic acid and muscimol affect the nervous system and can cause vomiting, confusion, agitation, hallucinations, delirium and seizures.

Fly agaric

Amanita muscaria · poisonous

Appearance to be aware of: familiar red to orange cap with white wart-like patches; white gills and stem. Colour and patches vary, and rain can remove patches.

Where: woodland and heath, often associated with birch or conifers; late summer to autumn.

Hazard: ibotenic acid and muscimol can cause nausea, confusion, altered perception, agitation, drowsiness and seizures. It is not safe to eat.

Appearance to be aware of: evergreen tree with flat needles and red, cup-shaped fleshy arils around a hard seed. It is not a true berry.

IF ANYONE MAY HAVE EATEN A WILD MUSHROOM, BERRY OR UNKNOWN PLANT Treat it as a possible poisoning. Do not wait for symptoms: some dangerous toxins have a delayed onset. In the UK, call 999 or go to A&E if you think someone may have swallowed something harmful. If you are unsure whether the substance is harmful, call NHS 111 immediately for urgent advice.

Immediate actions while help is arranged

  1. Stop further exposure. Move the person away from the plant or fungi. Remove any visible pieces from the mouth and rinse/spit only if they are fully alert and able to do so safely.
  2. Do not induce vomiting. Do not give food, drink, milk, alcohol, charcoal or home remedies unless a clinician or emergency call handler specifically instructs you.
  3. Preserve evidence. Photograph the whole plant/fungus, berries, leaves, cap, underside, stem and base. Keep a small remaining sample or packaging in a separate container if safe to do so; do not delay calling for help to collect it.
  4. Record the timeline. Note the likely species, time, estimated amount, number of people exposed and any symptoms. Keep the person under observation and follow medical instructions.
  5. Escalate immediately if they worsen. Collapse, confusion, seizure, severe or repeated vomiting, breathing difficulty or loss of consciousness means call 999. If unconscious but breathing normally, place them in the recovery position; if not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the call handler.
Important: touching a mushroom is not the same as eating it; most poisoning occurs through ingestion. Wash hands after handling unknown fungi or plants and avoid touching eyes or mouth. Do not destroy or collect wild plants unnecessarily.

13 / Navigation and getting found

  • Before leaving, note route, grid references, forecast, daylight, access points and a return time with someone reliable.
  • Carry a paper map and compass; protect the map from rain. Learn to orient it and take a bearing before you need to.
  • If lost: stop, stay calm, shelter if needed, check last known position and map. Avoid random movement, especially in poor visibility.
  • If safe, return along a known route. If not, call 999 and give grid reference, landmarks, phone location and battery level.
  • Do not cross fast rivers, railway lines, cliffs, quarries or private land to shorten a route.

Emergency signal

Use a phone first where possible. In remote areas, six repeated signals (whistle blasts, flashes or arm signals) followed by a pause is a recognised distress pattern in many outdoor settings. Make yourself visible in an open safe location; conserve phone battery.

14 / Weather, exposure and terrain

Cold and wetLayer clothing, keep dry, insulate from ground, replace wet layers, protect head/hands/feet. Watch for confusion and loss of coordination.
Heat and sunShade, cover skin, drink regularly, reduce exertion at peak heat. Confusion or collapse is heatstroke until proven otherwise — 999.
Wind and stormsLeave ridges and exposed high ground. Avoid lone trees, water, metal fences and open fields during lightning.
Floods and riversNever enter moving floodwater. Turn around; depth and current are deceptive. Avoid banks that may collapse.

15 / Rescue and emergency communications

What to tell 999

  • Exact location: grid reference, what3words if available, road/track, nearest access point and visible landmark.
  • What happened and when; number of casualties; age range if known.
  • Immediate hazards: violence, weapons, fire, water, electricity, traffic, unstable structures or animals.
  • Casualty condition: conscious? breathing normally? severe bleeding? trapped?
  • Your phone number and whether you can guide responders in. Do not hang up until told.

While waiting

  • Keep the scene safe and make access visible. Send a helper to meet responders if possible.
  • Keep the casualty warm and reassured. Recheck response and breathing regularly.
  • Do not remove helmets, embedded objects or protective equipment unless essential for airway/CPR or directed by a trained responder.
  • Preserve battery: dim screen, close unused apps, share location and call back if condition changes.

17 / Campcraft — build a safer base

Choose a site before unpacking. A good camp reduces exposure, protects supplies and makes every later task easier. In the UK, land access and wild-camping rights vary: use permitted sites, follow landowner rules and local restrictions, and leave no trace.

Read the groundChoose firm, naturally drained ground above flood level. Avoid dry stream beds, hollows, unstable banks, cliff edges, dead branches overhead, animal paths, bogs and exposed ridgelines. Never camp on fragile vegetation if a durable surface is available.
Read the windUse natural windbreaks without placing yourself under dead limbs. Face entrances away from prevailing wind where practical. Do not block ventilation in a shelter or tent.
Separate zonesKeep cooking, fuel, fire and waste away from sleeping areas. Store food sealed and away from wildlife. Keep a clear exit and do not string trip lines across walkways.
Ground insulationInsulate underneath before adding more blankets above. Use a sleeping mat, dry pack contents or purpose-made insulation. A waterproof sheet alone does not keep you warm.

Three shelter patterns

A-frame tarpGood general rain cover. Tension the ridge, peg corners and use a slight slope so water runs off. Keep enough headroom to avoid pressing against wet fabric.
Lean-toUseful as a windbreak in suitable conditions, but the open front offers limited protection from changing wind and rain. Add side protection or choose a more enclosed design if weather turns.
Natural debris shelterEmergency concept only. A debris shelter takes time, materials and energy; it can be damp, unstable and poor in severe weather. Do not damage live trees or strip protected habitat. A tarp and insulation are usually more reliable.

Build sequence

  1. Check land rules, weather, hazards and escape routes.
  2. Pick a site with drainage and natural protection, not a low hollow.
  3. Lay ground insulation and keep sleeping kit dry in a waterproof bag.
  4. Build the smallest shelter that fits the group and kit; secure it against likely wind.
  5. Check tension, anchors, runoff and ventilation. Recheck after gusts or rain.
  6. Keep cooking and any flame outside and well clear of fabric. Never use a stove or flame inside a tent or enclosed shelter.
Do not cut live branches, strip bark, dig trenches or build permanent structures. Avoid peat, nesting areas, protected habitats and fire-sensitive ground. Pack out all rubbish and dismantle temporary structures where required.

18 / Cordage, knots and lashings

Practise with thick rope in daylight before relying on knots with cold hands. A knot is only as reliable as the rope, material, dressing and final check. Never use improvised cordage for climbing, abseiling, life-safety loads or suspending a person.

Bowline — fixed loopCreates a fixed loop that should not tighten under ordinary hand tension. Learn the sequence slowly and inspect the finished knot; the diagram is conceptual, not a tying substitute.
Clove hitch — quick attachmentUseful for temporarily attaching a line to a post. It can slip on smooth, round or changing loads; add a suitable finishing hitch and check regularly. Not for life-safety use.
Taut-line / adjustable hitchAllows a guyline to be tensioned and adjusted. Tie on the standing part in the correct direction and test under gradual hand tension. Wet or slick cord may slip.
Figure-eight stopperA bulky stopper near a rope end that helps prevent it passing through a suitable opening. It is not a substitute for a secure attachment.

Square lashing — bind two poles

Safe cordage habits

  • Inspect for cuts, abrasion, UV damage, melted fibres and damaged stitching. Retire suspect cord.
  • Keep knots away from sharp edges and heat. Protect rope where it crosses rough surfaces.
  • Use wide, stable anchors. Do not trust rotten trees, loose rocks, fence posts or unknown structures.
  • Load-test shelters and lashings gradually by hand before placing people or valuable equipment beneath them.
  • Keep guy lines visible with bright markers to reduce trip hazards.

19 / Firecraft — ignition, fuel and control

Fire is a tool, not a requirement. In wet, windy or restricted conditions, a safe stove or extra insulation may be the better option. Check local fire restrictions and land rules first.

Prepare before striking

  1. Confirm a legal, safe site: bare mineral soil or an established fire area, clear of roots, peat, dry grass, overhanging branches, tents and fuel.
  2. Prepare three fuel sizes: fine tinder, pencil-thin kindling and progressively thicker dry sticks. Keep spare fuel dry and within reach but away from flame.
  3. Make a small, stable fire lay that allows airflow. Do not build a large pile that can collapse or throw sparks.
  4. Choose an ignition method you have practised: lighter, storm matches or ferrocerium rod. Shield tinder from wind without enclosing the flame.
  5. Feed small pieces gradually. If smoke increases or flame struggles, improve dry fuel and airflow rather than adding a large log.
  6. Keep water and a means of smothering ready. Never leave a fire unattended.

Ignition methods

Windproof lighterFast and simple; carry a spare in a separate waterproof place. Test before departure and protect from crushing or accidental activation.
Storm matchesStore dry in a sealed container with a separate striker. Use on a stable surface and extinguish the match fully.
Ferro rodScrape a small shower of sparks onto prepared fine tinder. Brace the rod close to tinder and pull the striker away so you do not scatter the bundle. Practise with dry tinder before relying on it.
Friction methodsBow-drill and hand-drill methods demand suitable dry materials, technique and energy. Learn with an instructor; do not depend on them in an emergency or use them where fire is restricted.

Fuel selection

  • Use only permitted, clean, untreated, dry wood. Avoid damp wood that produces excessive smoke.
  • Never burn painted, pressure-treated or glued wood, plastics, rubbish or unknown materials.
  • Do not strip live bark or cut live trees. Collect only where lawful and sustainable.
  • Do not use petrol, alcohol, aerosols or other accelerants. Never add fuel to a hot stove or fire.

Extinguish — cold, not just out

  1. Stop adding fuel and allow the fire to burn down under supervision.
  2. Douse slowly with water from a safe position, avoiding steam and splashing embers.
  3. Stir ashes and embers with a suitable tool; wet all sides and underneath.
  4. Repeat until no steam, smoke, glow or heat remains. Do not bury live embers.
  5. Check the surrounding ground and nearby roots. If you cannot confirm it is cold, keep treating it as live.
Never light a fire on peat, deep organic soil, during a fire ban or in strong gusts. Underground peat and root fires can smoulder unseen and spread. Use a stove or cold-camp instead.

20 / Watercraft — finding, collecting and treating water

Water planning is about both quantity and safety. Do not drink untreated water simply because it looks clear. Chemical contamination, salt and fuel are not made safe by boiling.

Source selection

  • Prefer sealed supplies or a known potable source. For surface water, choose upstream of camps, livestock, drains, roads and human activity where possible.
  • Avoid stagnant pools, algal blooms, scum, dead animals, industrial sites, fuel sheen, unusual colour or chemical smell. Do not collect near mine workings or contaminated land.
  • Rainwater can pick up contaminants from roofs, gutters, bird droppings and storage. Use a clean collection surface and treat appropriately.
  • Do not assume a stream is safe because it is fast-flowing, high up or visually clear.

Clarify, then disinfect

  1. Allow visible sediment to settle, then carefully pour clearer water into a clean vessel.
  2. Pass cloudy water through a clean cloth or suitable pre-filter. This removes particles, not pathogens.
  3. Bring water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute; above 2,000 m, boil for 3 minutes. Cool naturally in a covered clean vessel.
  4. Alternatively use a certified treatment product or filter exactly as its manufacturer specifies. Check which organisms it removes and whether it addresses viruses.
  5. Keep treated water separate from untreated water. Use clean hands and a clean, covered container.

Filter limitations

Portable microfilterCan reduce many bacteria and protozoa when correctly rated and maintained. Many do not remove viruses, salt or dissolved chemicals.
Activated carbonCan improve taste and reduce some chemicals depending on the product, but is not a reliable stand-alone disinfection method.
Cloth / coffee filterUseful only for removing grit and larger particles before treatment. It does not make water safe to drink.
Solar stillOutput is often very low and weather-dependent. It is not a dependable primary water plan; improvised stills may fail to separate contaminants reliably.
If chemical pollution, fuel, pesticides, heavy metals or salt are suspected, find another source. Boiling can concentrate some contaminants. Do not experiment with unknown purification chemicals or improvised dosing.

21 / Navigation practice — map, compass and terrain

A phone is useful, but it is not a navigation plan. Carry a current map and a baseplate compass, know how to use them and protect them from water. Practise locally before attempting remote routes.

Before setting off

  • Check map scale, legend, grid lines, contour interval, magnetic variation guidance, route restrictions and map date.
  • Mark start, destination, escape routes, water crossings, high ground, access points and decision points.
  • Check forecast, daylight, group ability, battery, clothing and turnaround time. Tell someone the route and overdue action.
  • Orient the map to the ground using terrain features. Keep it oriented as you move rather than constantly rotating your mental picture.

Use simple navigation techniques

HandrailFollow a long, obvious feature such as a path, wall, stream or ridge. Confirm it is the correct one at junctions. A stream may be dangerous to follow in steep or flooded terrain.
Attack pointNavigate to a large, unmistakable feature close to a small target. From there, use a short measured leg to the target.
Aiming offDeliberately aim to one side of a linear feature, so when you reach it you know which direction to turn. Do not aim directly at an indistinct crossing and guess.
PacingMeasure your own pace over known ground. Count double paces over a set distance, then adjust for slope, vegetation, mud and fatigue. Recalibrate with a loaded pack.
Back bearingUse the reciprocal bearing to check direction back to a known point. Add or subtract 180 degrees and keep the result within 000–360.
RelocationIf uncertain, stop and identify the last confirmed point. Use map, compass and multiple terrain features to relocate. Do not guess and compound the error.

Contour awareness

  • Close contour lines indicate steep ground; widely spaced contours indicate gentler slopes.
  • Contours form V-shapes in valleys and around spurs. Learn to identify which way water would flow.
  • Do not descend a steep valley or cliff line simply because it appears to point toward your destination.
  • In poor visibility, shorten legs and use confirmed features. If you cannot maintain a safe route, stop, shelter and reassess.

22 / Tools, cutting safety and field repairs

A small, maintained tool used correctly is more useful than a large collection of neglected equipment. Follow manufacturer instructions and local law; carry tools securely and use them only for legitimate camp tasks.

Knife and saw safety

  • Work at a stable height with a clear area. Keep other people outside the cutting arc and never cut toward your body, hand or leg.
  • Use a sharp, suitable tool: blunt blades require more force and are more likely to slip. Keep fingers behind the cutting edge.
  • Use controlled strokes and stop when tired, cold, distracted or rushed. Wear eye protection when sawing or splitting.
  • Close or sheath tools immediately after use. Store them so they cannot move around in a pack.
  • Do not throw knives, baton large logs with an unsuitable blade, or use a knife as a pry bar, screwdriver or hammer.
  • Use a saw for branches where permitted rather than forcing a knife through large timber. Do not cut live trees or protected vegetation.

Basic field repairs

Torn fabricClean and dry the area, bring edges together without stretching, apply repair tape or a compatible patch to both sides if possible. Round patch corners to reduce peeling.
Broken strapUnload it first. Use a spare buckle, webbing repair or temporary lashing only for non-life-safety loads. Replace damaged load-bearing parts before carrying heavy loads.
Wet footwearRemove insoles, loosen laces, dry gradually with airflow and change socks. Do not place boots against a fire or intense heater; heat can damage materials and cause burns.
Damaged guylineReplace with sound cord and secure to a reliable anchor. Keep the line visible and check tension after wind changes.
Leaking bottleTransfer water to a clean intact container. Tape may be a temporary external fix only if it does not contaminate drinking water; replace the vessel.
Battery managementUse low-power mode, dim the screen, close unused apps, keep the phone warm and store a power bank dry. Keep a paper map and compass as independent backups.

Maintenance routine

  1. Clean and dry tools after use; remove sap, grit and moisture.
  2. Check handles, locks, hinges, edges, stitching and fasteners before each trip.
  3. Oil or maintain tools only as their manufacturer directs. Keep lubricants away from food and drinking vessels.
  4. Replace damaged safety equipment rather than improvising a repair to something that must hold a person or stop a fall.

23 / Longer stays — routines, stores and group management

For a longer delay, a simple routine prevents small problems becoming serious. Do not turn an emergency bivouac into a permanent camp where land rules or conditions make that unsafe.

Morning checkWeather, people, injuries, water stock, fuel, battery, shelter tension, route and daylight. Set a clear plan for the day.
Water disciplineLabel untreated and treated containers. Keep a reserve for cooking and first aid. Track how much is used and how much remains.
Food disciplineKeep a simple inventory, protect food from animals, separate raw and cooked items and use perishable supplies first when safely stored.
Waste controlPack out rubbish, keep waste away from water and food, and wash hands after handling it. Do not burn plastics or bury non-biodegradable waste.
Group rolesAgree who monitors weather, water, cooking, navigation and first aid. Use buddy checks and make sure everyone knows the plan.
Rest and moraleSchedule rest, keep clothing dry, rotate tasks fairly and maintain a calm routine. Fatigue causes accidents and poor decisions.

Daily decision board

Food and energy priorities

  • Use known safe, sealed food before attempting foraging. Keep portions sensible and avoid unnecessary exertion while hungry or dehydrated.
  • Do not rely on wild plants, fungi, insects or unverified recipes as a food plan. Identification and contamination risks are real.
  • Keep cooking fuel for safe food preparation and water treatment where appropriate. Never cook in a closed shelter.
  • Do not ration water to the point of dehydration. Reduce heat exposure and exertion, and seek a safe supply.
If someone becomes confused, collapses, cannot keep fluids down, stops urinating normally or shows signs of severe dehydration or heat illness, treat it as a medical problem—not a camp-management issue.

24 / Signalling, visibility and finding your way back

Make yourself easy to locate without putting yourself in more danger. Signal from a safe, visible position and keep a record of what you have tried.

WhistleUse three repeated blasts, pause and listen. Repeat at intervals. A whistle carries farther than shouting and costs less energy.
TorchUse repeated groups of flashes with pauses. Preserve battery; do not stare into or aim a powerful beam at aircraft or drivers.
Visual contrastUse a bright panel, high-visibility clothing or a ground marker in a clear open area. Keep clear of roads, power lines and helicopter landing hazards.
Sound and movementStay in one safe, known location when searchers are likely to be looking. Respond to calls and signals; avoid moving into dense cover or hazardous terrain.

Search and rendezvous discipline

  • Agree a meeting point and return time before anyone leaves camp. Do not split a group casually.
  • If a person is missing, note their last known location, clothing, equipment, direction and time. Preserve the last known point.
  • Do not send untrained people into hazardous terrain, fast water, cliffs or darkness. Keep a safe person at the rendezvous point.
  • Use a written log of signals, sightings, weather and movement. Avoid repeatedly changing location.
  • If you hear aircraft or search teams, make yourself visible from a safe open area and follow their directions.
